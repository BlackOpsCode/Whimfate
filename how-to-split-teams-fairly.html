TEAMWORK · LOW-DRAMA LOGISTICS

How to split teams without creating lore.

The fastest way to get a friendly game started is usually not a live draft with captains, lobbying, and a surprisingly intense debate about Dave.

4 MIN READ · WHIMFATE FIELD NOTES

There are two ways to split a group into teams. One is to shuffle the names. The other is to appoint captains, watch them select their friends, and accidentally recreate a playground documentary. If your goal is to start the game without producing a lifelong grudge, a little structure helps.

Start with the actual goal

For casual games, a random split is often the fastest way to keep the process neutral. For competitive games, you may need to balance experience, roles, or ability first. Randomness can distribute people who are already roughly balanced; it cannot guarantee a fair contest if one team has every experienced player.

Count before you shuffle

Know how many players you have and how many teams you want. If the numbers don't divide evenly, decide whether one team gets an extra person or whether a substitute/rotation makes more sense. Better to agree on this now than invent a constitutional amendment in front of everyone.

Keep the input list clean

One name per line is easy to check. Remove duplicates, agree on how to handle late arrivals, and make sure everyone's included. If the list contains “Dave” twice, that may be two Daves — or a copy-paste cry for help.

Shuffle once, then show the result

For a friendly game, a single transparent shuffle is usually enough. Show the teams clearly and make copying the result easy. Re-rolling over and over because someone doesn't like their team makes the process feel less neutral. If there is a real balancing issue, adjust the rules up front rather than secretly rerolling until the universe agrees with the captain.

Make room for practical constraints

Some activities need specific roles, accessibility accommodations, or safe group sizes. Handle those constraints first, then randomize within the remaining options. Fairness is not always the same thing as pretending everyone is interchangeable.

And then play the game

The ideal team splitter has a short life: open it, enter the names, split the group, share the result, start playing. It should not become the pre-game game, complete with lobby politics and a recap podcast.
